Research

Mechanics, simulation, and machine learning for engineering systems.

Mechanics describes how materials and structures respond to load, heat, and deformation, and the finite element method turns that description into engineering predictions. Machine learning adds something new: models that learn directly from data.

In practice, though, most data-driven models use mechanics and simulation only to generate training data, not as part of the model itself. This becomes a problem when engineering data are sparse, noisy, or only indirectly related to the quantity we care about.

My research builds governing equations and numerical structure into the learning itself, so that the models stay physically consistent even with very little data. I have used this to discover hyperelastic constitutive laws from a single test, to infer thermoelectric and flexoelectric properties without property labels, and to build surrogates that work for materials and geometries they were not trained on.

Looking ahead, I want simulations that can be trusted in real time. A prediction is only useful for an engineering decision if we also know how confident it is, so I see physics-informed modeling and uncertainty quantification as two parts of the same goal: digital twins that are fast, physically consistent, and honest about their uncertainty.

Current Focus

These are the topics I am working on now:

Mechanics-Based Material & Structural Modeling
Constitutive theory and finite element modeling describe how materials and structures respond to mechanical, thermal, and coupled loading. This is the base of everything else I do: it fixes the governing equations, boundary conditions, and discretization that any learned model has to respect. Without it, a data-driven model has nothing to stay consistent with.
Related work  [12] flexoelectric origin of frictional contrast on ferroelectrics (Science Advances, 2026) · [11] energy-based flexoelectric framework (IJNME, 2026) · [16] hybrid GNN–FEM for phase-field fracture (Eng. Fract. Mech., 2027)
Physics-Informed Machine Learning
I build governing equations and numerical structure into neural network training, using physics-informed neural networks, neural operators, and differentiable simulation. Because the physics limits which solutions are allowed, these models need far less data and behave more sensibly outside the training range. This matters most when experiments or high-fidelity simulations are expensive.
Related work  [5] hyperelastic law discovery from scarce data (CMAME, 2025) · [6] label-free thermoelectric inference with neural operators (npj Comput. Mater., 2025) · [15] differentiable FEM vs. PINN for inverse analysis (CACIE, 2026) · [9] perspective on PIML for material characterization (2025)
Reduced-Order Modeling for Real-Time Simulation
Reduced-order models compress a high-fidelity simulation into a few modes that run orders of magnitude faster while keeping its physical structure. I work on intrusive and stochastic versions of these models, because applications such as monitoring and control need answers faster than a full simulation can give them.
Related work  [16] physics-regularized GNN–FEM surrogate that generalizes across geometries and meshes (Eng. Fract. Mech., 2027)
Uncertainty Quantification for Trustworthy Prediction
An engineering decision needs not only a prediction but also a sense of how much to trust it. I work on estimating that confidence from the model itself. In structural health monitoring and digital twins, where an overconfident wrong answer can be costly, knowing when not to trust a model matters as much as the prediction.
