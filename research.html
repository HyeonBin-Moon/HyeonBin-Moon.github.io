Research

AI for solid mechanics — letting physics carry the information that engineering data lacks, toward trustworthy, real-time digital twins.

Mechanics supplies simulation with its governing equations, and simulation supplies data-driven methods with a benchmark for physical validity. Purely data-driven approaches, however, tend to treat mechanics and simulation only as sources of training data rather than as structural priors for the model itself. This becomes a real limitation when engineering data is sparse, noisy, or indirect relative to the complexity of the systems it describes.

My research sits at the intersection of AI and solid mechanics, and in it solid mechanics is the physics in physics-informed learning: constitutive theory, energy principles, and finite-element structure are embedded directly into the learning framework, rather than relying on data alone. Across my work, the recurring question is where to put the physics — in the loss through weak-form and energy principles, in the structure of the model through candidate laws, convexity, and hard boundary conditions, or in the solver itself through finite-element coupling — and each choice determines how far a model can be trusted beyond its data.

Long-term, my aim is to close the gap between simulation and the real world, in both predictive accuracy and trustworthiness. A prediction without a quantified degree of trust is of limited use for real engineering decisions. I work toward a research direction in which physics-informed modeling and uncertainty quantification are treated as inseparable, enabling real-time, physically consistent digital twins that can be deployed with confidence in high-stakes engineering systems.

Research Thrusts

Physics-Informed Material Identification

Can constitutive laws and material properties be identified from only a handful of noisy measurements?

Material laws are rarely measured directly; they must be inferred from a few displacements, forces, or temperatures. With solid mechanics as the physics in physics-informed learning — weak-form and energy-based losses on finite-element discretizations, candidate-function libraries with sparse regression for interpretable laws, and hard-constrained boundary conditions — a hyperelastic constitutive model is recovered from a single test even under 3% noise; temperature-dependent thermoelectric properties are inferred without property labels, and with neural operators for unseen materials without retraining; and flexoelectric coefficients are identified from sparse data without evaluating fourth-order derivatives. I also examine when these methods should not be used: for layered-pavement inverse analysis, physics-informed neural networks fail at sharp material discontinuities, while a differentiable finite element formulation that enforces the physics as a hard constraint is more accurate, robust, and efficient.

Multiphysics Mechanics of Materials

Which coupled-field mechanics explains what experiments observe?

Many material phenomena arise from coupled fields. Using continuum electromechanical modeling, I showed that the asymmetric friction observed on ferroelectric surfaces originates from flexoelectrically induced polarization competing with or enhancing the ferroelectric polarization — the basis for fast, voltage-free imaging of ferroelectric domains. The same flexoelectric coupling then became a target for forward and inverse analysis in my physics-informed framework. For history-dependent phase-field fracture, a hybrid GNN–FEM framework replaces only the phase-field update with a graph neural network while the finite element solver keeps equilibrium and boundary conditions; it lowers computational cost and generalizes to unseen geometries, loadings, material properties, and meshes.

Agentic AI for Scientific Problem Solving

Can self-correcting AI agents carry out validated scientific problem solving — starting with physics simulation — without expert intervention?

MCP-SIM, a self-correcting multi-agent LLM framework, turns underspecified natural-language prompts into validated finite-element simulations and explanatory reports. Instead of one-shot code generation, it reasons through plan–act–reflect–revise cycles with a shared memory, and solved all twelve benchmark tasks — including thermoelectric coupling, piezoelectricity, and phase-field fracture.
Key work  [10] MCP-SIM (npj Artificial Intelligence, 2026) · [7] LLM interfaces in knowledge-guided inverse design (2025)
Inverse Design for Manufacturing

Can a target shape or performance be turned back into a design quickly and reliably?

My M.S. work automated line-heating design for ship hull plates: a DNN surrogate trained on seven million FEM samples, combined with multi-start convex optimization, designs heating patterns for unseen plate shapes in under 30 seconds with about 1–2% error. Building on this, I argued that AI inverse design in manufacturing needs three pillars — domain knowledge to define the problem, physics-informed learning to generalize under sparse and biased data, and language-model interfaces for human–AI collaboration.

What's Next — Toward Trustworthy, Real-Time Digital Twins

Two limitations recur across my published work. The identified properties have so far been validated on simulated data rather than physical experiments, and the inferences are deterministic — my flexoelectric framework lists noise-aware formulations as the open step, and my perspective on physics-informed characterization names probabilistic models as a priority for the field. My next steps address both, while growing three directions together — mechanics modeling, physics-informed learning, and digital twins:

  • Mechanics modeling across scales and time — temporal homogenization of thermo-viscoelastic materials and constitutive modeling of soft active materials such as liquid-crystal-elastomer artificial muscles.
  • Closing the loop with experiments — completing the identification story with plasticity discovered from hole-expansion experiments: a proof of concept that the laws identified by physics-informed learning hold up on real measurements.
  • Near-real-time system parameter estimation — a digital twin must keep pace with the system it mirrors. Bringing my experience in inverse identification of material properties to whole systems, I estimate the parameters of a digital twin from sparse sensors, using stochastic reduced-order models to reach near-real-time speed.
  • Stochastic reduced-order models for real-time simulation with UQ — reduced-order modeling is what makes a digital twin fast enough; reduced bases that evolve on the Grassmann manifold keep the model valid, and its uncertainty estimate honest, as the system state changes.

How the Pieces Fit

The thrusts above build a stack, from a mechanics foundation to trustworthy real-time prediction:

Mechanics-Based Material & Structural ModelingEstablished
Finite element modeling and constitutive theory form the physical basis for describing how materials and structures respond to mechanical, thermal, and coupled multiphysics loading. This work defines the governing equations, boundary conditions, and numerical discretization that any downstream model must remain consistent with. It plays the role of a foundation layer: every learning-based method further down this list inherits its physical validity from the accuracy of this mechanics description. Without it, data-driven components would have no structure to be grounded in, and predictions would reduce to pattern-matching with no guarantee of physical plausibility.
Physics-Informed Machine LearningEstablished
Governing equations and numerical structure are embedded directly into neural network training — through physics-informed neural networks and differentiable simulation — rather than learned solely from data. This method serves as the bridge between the mechanics foundation above and practical prediction: it allows a model to be trained on far less data than a purely data-driven counterpart would require, because the physics itself constrains the space of admissible solutions. This is essential in engineering settings where high-fidelity experiments or simulations are expensive to generate, and where extrapolating safely beyond observed conditions matters more than fitting them well.
Reduced-Order Modeling for Real-Time Digital TwinsNext
Intrusive and stochastic reduced-order models compress high-fidelity simulations into low-dimensional representations that run orders of magnitude faster, while preserving the underlying physical structure of the full-order model. This addresses a practical bottleneck: physically accurate simulations are often too computationally expensive to run in real time, which makes them unusable for applications requiring immediate feedback, such as monitoring or control. Reduced-order modeling exists precisely to resolve this tension between fidelity and speed, making real-time deployment possible without discarding the physics that makes predictions trustworthy.
Uncertainty Quantification for Trustworthy PredictionNext
A prediction alone is not enough to support a real engineering decision — it must come with a quantified degree of confidence attached to it. This work focuses on developing frameworks that estimate that confidence directly from the model, so that a result is not just a number but a statement of how much that number can be trusted. This role is what converts a model's output into information that can support a decision. It is indispensable in applications such as structural health monitoring and digital twins, where the cost of an overconfident wrong prediction can be severe, and where knowing when a model should not be trusted is as important as the prediction itself.
