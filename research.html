Research

Bringing mechanics, simulation, and machine learning together for real engineering systems.

Mechanics gives us the language for how materials and structures respond to load, heat, and deformation. The finite element method turned that language into predictive engineering tools, and machine learning now adds a third capability: learning directly from data.

These three build on one another — mechanics supplies the governing equations, and simulation supplies a benchmark for physical validity. Yet purely data-driven models usually treat mechanics and simulation only as sources of training data, not as structure inside the model. That becomes a real limitation when engineering data is sparse, noisy, or only indirectly related to what we want to know.

My research closes this gap by building governing equations and numerical structure directly into the learning framework. Grounded in mechanics and numerical analysis, these physics-informed models stay physically consistent and dependable even when data is severely limited.

In the long run, I want to narrow the distance between simulation and the real world in both accuracy and trust. A prediction without a measure of confidence is of limited use for an engineering decision, so I treat physics-informed modeling and uncertainty quantification as inseparable — toward real-time, physically consistent digital twins that can be deployed with confidence.

Current Focus

I'm currently focusing on the following:

Mechanics-Based Material & Structural Modeling
Constitutive theory and finite element modeling describe how materials and structures respond to mechanical, thermal, and coupled multiphysics loading. This is the foundation layer: it fixes the governing equations, boundary conditions, and discretization that every learning-based model further down must stay consistent with. Without it, data-driven components would have nothing to be grounded in, and predictions would reduce to pattern matching with no guarantee of physical plausibility.

Physics-Informed Machine Learning
Governing equations and numerical structure are built directly into neural network training — through physics-informed neural networks, neural operators, and differentiable simulation — rather than learned from data alone. This is the bridge from the mechanics foundation to practical prediction: because the physics constrains the space of admissible solutions, a model can be trained on far less data and extrapolates more safely. It matters most where experiments or high-fidelity simulations are expensive, and where behaving well outside the observed conditions matters more than fitting them.

Reduced-Order Modeling for Real-Time Simulation
Intrusive and stochastic reduced-order models compress high-fidelity simulations into low-dimensional representations that run orders of magnitude faster while keeping the physical structure of the full model. They resolve a practical bottleneck: accurate simulations are often too slow for applications that need immediate feedback, such as monitoring or control. Reduced-order modeling makes real-time deployment possible without discarding the physics that makes a prediction trustworthy.
Related work  [16] physics-regularized GNN–FEM surrogate that generalizes across geometries and meshes (Eng. Fract. Mech., 2027)
Uncertainty Quantification for Trustworthy Prediction
A prediction alone cannot support an engineering decision; it needs a quantified degree of confidence. This work develops frameworks that estimate that confidence from the model itself, so that a result is not just a number but a statement of how far the number can be trusted. In structural health monitoring and digital twins, where an overconfident wrong answer can be costly, knowing when not to trust a model is as important as the prediction itself.
